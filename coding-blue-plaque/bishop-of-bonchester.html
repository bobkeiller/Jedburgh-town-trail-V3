A death before the royal wedding

On the night before the 1285 wedding of Alexander III and Princess Yolande at Jedburgh Abbey, the Bishop of Bonchester was found slain in his chamber. His blood-covered tunic suggested that he had been stabbed.

The news was kept quiet so it would not spoil the wedding. Some later claimed that the apparition seen during the ceremony was the murdered bishop’s ghost.

The Bishop was stern, bullying and corrupt. Many people had reason to resent him.

1280Worship taxOne penny to attend church—and two for failing to appear.
1278Marriage conditionHe refused to marry couples unless the bride-to-be spent a night with him.
1276Livestock levyFarmers had to donate one animal in every 22 to the Church.
1274Drink banAlcohol sales were banned in Bonchester, Chester, Southdean and Bedrule.

Evidence

  1. The chamber guard checked the room for waiting assailants before admitting the Bishop.
  2. A person tending horses at the Glebe was described as broad and tall, with long blond hair.
  3. Sir George Kerr bought everyone a drink at the Carter’s Rest on the night before the wedding.
  4. Harriet wanted to marry in Bonchester but refused the Bishop’s condition introduced in 1278.
  5. Sir George liked a drink and resented the local ban imposed in 1274.
  6. The red-haired suspect arrived in Jedburgh on the wedding morning. His servants confirmed it. He also hated paying the worship tax.
  7. A witness saw a tall, long-haired person near the Bishop’s chamber. In the darkness, they could not tell whether it was a man or a woman.
  8. The stone-floor access hatch, installed by Harkins, did not fit properly and could not be opened.
  9. The person whose grievance began in 1276 was too large to fit through the window.
  10. The guard arrived with the Bishop, checked the room, remained at his post all night and saw no one approach the door.

Correct—Harriet Hamilton killed the Bishop

Harriet climbed through the window. She had no evidence to disprove her involvement and simply denied it.

The witness narrowed the intruder to long-haired Oxnam or Harriet. The guard’s evidence eliminated the door and lying in wait, while the defective hatch eliminated the floor route. That left the window. Oxnam—the 1276 livestock critic—was too large to fit through it, leaving Harriet as the only possible culprit.
